Neon Blocks

A versus block-stacking game where you race an AI opponent or another player, sending garbage lines and skill attacks to top them out.

My role
Solo developer
Team
1 person (everything from code to UI)
Engine and language
Unity, C#, Photon Fusion
Timeframe
1 month

What I built

  • An AI opponent that plays its own board: it searches every rotation and column for the current piece and scores each result with a well-known weighted heuristic (stack height, holes, bumpiness and lines cleared).
  • Four difficulty levels tuned by think time, input speed and a mistake chance that sometimes picks one of the top five alternatives. The AI also gets faster every minute, down to a set limit.
  • The AI sends inputs through the same input struct as a human player, so it follows identical rules and can’t cheat. In networked matches it runs on the host.
  • A skill point system with three attack tiers that both the player and the AI can spend.

Problem I solved

My first opponent had no board. It fired random attacks on a timer, which felt unfair and gave the player nothing to read or react to. I rebuilt it to play a real board with the same controls as the player, and made difficulty come from slower reactions and imperfect choices instead of special rules. Now losing to the AI feels like losing to a person.

Design decisions

Skills as untapped space. Mainstream block-stacking games are almost entirely about stacking speed, and active abilities are a direction they have barely explored. Skills give casual players a second way to win, so a well-timed attack can matter as much as fast hands.

Cost matches impact. Each skill costs more the more it disrupts the opponent’s board. The cheapest removes a single block, the middle tier forces an awkward Z piece, and the most expensive drops an X bomb that is very hard to fit. Players learn to choose between spending early on small attacks or saving up for one that can end the match.

Keeping the AI beatable. A placement-scoring AI plays close to perfectly, and casual players stop having fun when they can’t win. I gave it human limits instead: slower reactions, a chance to choose a weaker move on lower difficulties, and a gradual speed-up so every match builds tension instead of starting at full pressure.

Source code and a playable build are available on request for interviews.

UniBoom 3D

A turn-based artillery game in 3D. Pick a class, explore the world map, then aim, charge and fire across 3D terrain against monsters and a dragon boss.

My role
Gameplay programmer
Team
2 people
Engine and tools
Unity 6, C#, Cinemachine, PlayFab
Timeframe
7 months, capstone project

What I built

  • Game flow: the path from the world map and NPC quests into battle, the turn order between the player’s team and the enemies, and win, lose and wave transitions.
  • The shooting system: angle and power aiming in 3D, shells that fly on a real arc, raycast hit detection so fast shots never pass through thin objects, and area damage that hits each target only once.
  • Enemy AI that takes turns one by one and aims with adjustable error, so each shot either hits or lands just short.
  • The skill system: six skills, from rocket barrages and fire attacks to heal and shield, paid for with turn energy.

Design decision

An AI that calculates the perfect arc every turn would win every match and feel unfair. I gave its aim tunable variables, so enemies sometimes land a direct hit and sometimes just miss. Near misses keep the player under pressure while leaving room to recover, which makes each enemy turn tense instead of predictable.

Tech demo built as my graduation capstone at VTC Academy. A playable build is available on request.
